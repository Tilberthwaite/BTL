Executive summary

Barrow-in-Furness sits at an unusual intersection: a large, well-paid, mobile workforce (BAE Systems Dreadnought programme — £31bn, through the 2030s; plus AUKUS) combined with residential property prices well below the national average. This produces rental yields that are exceptional by UK standards for disciplined auction buyers.

The BRRR cycle — Buy at auction, Refurbish, Refinance, Rent — allows one £100,000 investment to fund multiple properties in sequence. The 75% LTV mortgage on a £130,000 post-refurb value returns £97,500 — repaying the £75,000 all-in cost and leaving a £22,500 surplus for the next acquisition. After Property 1 refinances (~Month 5), the cycle is substantially self-funding.

At current market rates (5.25% IO, Ltd company 5yr fix, April 2026), the monthly mortgage on a £97,500 loan is £427/month — 47% of the £900 rent from a 4-bed property. Net cashflow in Year 1 is £248/month per property. This is correct for interest-only BTL.

The mechanic: the mortgage is fixed for 5 years while rent grows 5–7%/year (ONS: W&F rents rose 7.1% in 12 months to Dec 2025). The same property nets £248/month in Year 1 and £406/month in Year 5 from the same fixed mortgage. Every penny of rent growth is pure profit. Inflation works the same way — it erodes the real cost of the fixed mortgage each year.

Director’s income — growing beyond £2,000 and beating inflation

£2,000/month in today’s money loses purchasing power every year. At 3% annual inflation, maintaining the same standard of living requires £2,319/month by Year 5, £2,534 by Year 8, and £2,688 by Year 10. The bar charts below show actual income against this rising target. The red marker is the inflation-adjusted target — income must reach past it to maintain real purchasing power.

Why income grows faster than inflation: (1) Rent rises 5–7%/yr on all properties. (2) Each new property multiplies total income. (3) The fixed mortgage costs nothing extra as inflation rises. By Year 10, the £427/month mortgage on Property 1 is worth significantly less in real terms — inflation is quietly subsidising the model every year.
